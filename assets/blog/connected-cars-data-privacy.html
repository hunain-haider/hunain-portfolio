Connected Cars Are Sharing Your Data: What a New Study Reveals

Modern connected vehicles can communicate with manufacturers, analytics platforms, advertisers and other third-party services. New research offers a closer look at how that digital ecosystem works.

✦ Written by Hunain Haider Automotive Technology September 2026 10 min read
The Connected-Car Privacy Problem: Modern vehicles are increasingly becoming internet-connected computing platforms. A study by Northeastern University researchers, conducted with Consumer Reports, examined 21 vehicles from 19 brands and 30 companion apps to understand where connected-car data travels.

1. What Did the Study Examine?

Researchers from Northeastern University, working with Consumer Reports, investigated the network behavior of connected vehicles rather than relying only on what manufacturers describe in privacy policies.

The team tested 21 vehicles from 19 different brands under several conditions, including when vehicles were stationary, while they were being driven, and while their infotainment systems were being used.

The researchers also examined 30 companion smartphone applications used by connected-car owners. The goal was to understand the wider digital ecosystem surrounding modern vehicles.

Why Network Traffic Matters

Much of the traffic was encrypted, so the researchers could not read every individual data packet. However, network traces still revealed domains contacted through DNS and TLS information, transmission timing, traffic volume and differences between testing scenarios.

For electric vehicles, the researchers also used a Faraday enclosure to block cellular signals and observe whether some vehicles redirected their traffic through Wi-Fi.

2. Cars Were Contacting Third-Party Domains

Every connected vehicle in the study contacted a domain belonging to its automaker. That is expected because connected services need to communicate with manufacturer-controlled infrastructure.

The more notable finding was that some vehicles also contacted numerous third-party domains associated with advertising, analytics, tracking, entertainment and mapping services.

Alphabet/Google domains appeared frequently in the observed traffic. The researchers linked this in part to the growing presence of Google's automotive software and services in connected vehicles.

A Large Digital Footprint

Reporting on the study found that the Tesla Model 3 contacted 34 advertising, tracking and analytics domains, along with 37 domains associated with applications integrated into its infotainment system.

A third-party connection does not automatically prove that personal information is being misused. Some external services are necessary for legitimate vehicle functions. The research instead demonstrates how many external services can become part of a connected vehicle's network ecosystem.

3. Infotainment Systems Add More Connections

Interacting with a vehicle's infotainment system generally resulted in more domain contacts than simply leaving a vehicle idle or driving it without using the infotainment features.

Modern infotainment platforms can combine navigation, music streaming, mapping, voice services, software platforms and other online features. Each service can introduce additional network communications.

This means the privacy profile of a connected vehicle can change depending on how the driver uses its digital features.

4. Companion Apps Add Another Tracking Layer

The vehicle itself is only one part of the connected-car ecosystem. Smartphone applications can provide remote controls, vehicle status, location features and other services.

The researchers found that some of the tested apps also communicated with advertising and analytics companies.

Northeastern University reported that the observed data flows included information such as vehicle identification numbers (VINs), email addresses and location information in some cases.

Companies identified in the research included Adobe Analytics, Axiom and ContentSquare among other third-party services.

Why App Permissions Matter

A connected-car app can provide useful remote features, but the app may also have access to information that is more directly associated with a driver's account or smartphone. Reviewing app permissions is therefore an important part of understanding vehicle privacy.

5. Why Location Data Matters

Location information can reveal patterns over time. Repeated location records can potentially indicate where someone lives, works, shops or regularly travels.

When location information is combined with identifiers such as an email address or vehicle identification number, it can create a more detailed digital profile.

Honda's published connected-product privacy documentation describes categories of information that can include VINs, vehicle operation information, trip information, geolocation and other usage-related information.

Data Category Why It Can Matter
Location Can reveal travel patterns and frequently visited places.
VIN Provides a persistent identifier associated with a vehicle.
Email / Account Data Can connect vehicle-related activity to an account.
Trip Information Can describe journeys and vehicle usage patterns.

6. What Happened With Honda?

One of the clearest changes reported after the research involved Honda's connected-car application.

According to Northeastern University, researchers found that Honda's application was sending location information and VINs to analytics company Amplitude.

After the researchers raised the issue, Honda asked Amplitude to delete the location data it had received and updated the application so that it would stop sending geolocation information to the company.

Honda spokesperson Andrew Quillin confirmed the researchers' assessment to Northeastern Global News.

Honda's Explanation

Honda said contractual restrictions prevented the shared VIN and location information from being independently used or sold. Honda also stated that use of the connected application is voluntary.

The Honda example is important because it shows the difference between identifying a network data flow and determining how a company says that information may be used. Both the technical finding and the company's explanation are relevant to understanding the issue.

7. How Automakers Responded

Automakers generally point to privacy policies and contractual restrictions when explaining relationships with third-party service providers.

Researchers said that privacy policies can disclose third-party sharing without always making it easy for consumers to identify every company receiving information or understand exactly why a particular data flow exists.

There is also a practical challenge for consumers: declining certain data-sharing permissions can sometimes mean losing access to connected features.

This creates a difficult choice for drivers who purchased a vehicle partly because of its connected features but do not want to share more information than necessary.

8. Cars Are Becoming Data Platforms

Modern vehicles increasingly combine hardware, software, cloud services and smartphone applications. A connected car can include:

  • GPS and navigation systems
  • Cellular connectivity
  • Wi-Fi and Bluetooth
  • Voice assistants
  • Infotainment applications
  • Remote vehicle-control services
  • Cloud-based vehicle services
  • Smartphone companion applications
  • Cameras and other vehicle sensors

This makes the vehicle less like a standalone machine and more like a networked computing platform. As a result, understanding car privacy increasingly requires looking at the complete ecosystem rather than only the automaker.

9. What Can Car Owners Do?

Completely eliminating data collection may not be possible when using connected services, but owners can take practical steps to reduce unnecessary exposure.

Review Vehicle Privacy Settings

Check the car's privacy and data-sharing controls instead of automatically accepting every option.

Review Companion-App Permissions

Check what information the manufacturer's mobile app can access, particularly location and account-related permissions.

Read the Important Parts of the Privacy Policy

Look specifically for location data, vehicle information, analytics, advertising and third-party sharing.

Disable Services You Do Not Need

If a connected feature is unnecessary, disabling it may reduce the amount of information being transmitted.

Reset the Vehicle Before Selling It

Remove personal accounts, paired devices and stored information before transferring ownership. Honda's documentation also describes procedures for removing personal information from connected systems.

10. Conclusion

Connected-car technology provides useful features such as remote controls, navigation, vehicle status information, entertainment and cloud-based services. But those features also turn the vehicle into a participant in a much larger digital ecosystem.

The Northeastern University and Consumer Reports research shows that modern vehicles and their companion applications can communicate with a wide range of external domains and services.

The Honda case also demonstrates that specific data-sharing practices can change after researchers identify a particular data flow. For drivers, the central issue is not simply whether a car is connected, but what information is being transmitted, who receives it, why it is needed and how much control the owner has over that information.

Bottom line: A connected car is more than a vehicle with internet access. It is part of a digital ecosystem where information can move between the vehicle, its manufacturer, smartphone applications and third-party services.
